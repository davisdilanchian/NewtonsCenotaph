Cénotaphe de Newton

Étienne-Louis Boullée (1728–1799) drew buildings on a scale so grand they seemed made to be impossible, and a screen may be the only place they will ever stand. He is best known, fittingly, for one that was never built: this cenotaph for Isaac Newton (1643–1727), drawn in 1784. It is a hollow sphere a little over 150 m high, taller than the Great Pyramid of Giza, ringed by hundreds of cypresses, its vault pierced so that daylight falls through as stars.

It is rebuilt here from his surviving drawings, with Opus 5.5. At this scale they show little fine detail, so some is my own, most of all in the night-sky chamber, where the openings follow the real sky over Woolsthorpe on the night Newton was born.

Best on a large monitor. On a phone, hold it out at arm’s length and turn: the view follows, like a window into the place.

The music is the opening of Haydn’s Creation (1798): chaos, and then “Let there be light”, the words Alexander Pope turned into Newton’s epitaph: “Nature and Nature’s laws lay hid in night; God said, Let Newton be! and all was light.” Sound on.

Published accounts put the sphere at 150 m.
